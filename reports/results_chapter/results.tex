\chapter{Results}

\section{Scope and evidence base}\label{sec:res-orient}

This chapter reports what three environments of increasing favourability to the agent
returned. Every verdict below was produced on a block of simulated or replayed market data
that was sealed until the decision rule had been fixed, and every decision rule was
registered before the data were seen.

Sections~\ref{sec:res-replay} to~\ref{sec:res-comparators} answer Research Question~1. Each is
placed where it is because the preceding result invites a specific objection, and the
section answers it. Section~\ref{sec:res-learned} answers Research Question~3,
Section~\ref{sec:res-regime} Research Question~2.

% =====================================================================================
\section{Execution performance on recorded order books}\label{sec:res-replay}

Seventy agents were trained on three years of Hyperliquid BTC--USD order books replayed at
one-minute and ten-second resolution: two algorithms, three order sizes from 0.5\% to 2\% of
average daily volume, two execution deadlines, five random seeds per configuration,
fourteen configurations in all. All seventy were then evaluated once, together, on a test
period that had never been touched.

The verdict is \emph{no stable edge} (Table~\ref{tab:t7}). Four of the fourteen
configurations did clear the
pre-registered bar, and Section~\ref{sec:res-apparent} returns to them, because what they
turn out to be is more informative than the null itself. Across the whole exam the agents
neither systematically beat nor systematically lost to TWAP, and no configuration's result
survived a change of evaluation period.

This environment has a clear limitation, and it is the reason the study does not stop here:
replayed books cannot respond. An agent whose orders would in reality have moved the price
against it is not penalised for that here, so any apparent skill could be an artefact of
frictionless execution.

% =====================================================================================
\section{Execution performance in a reacting market}\label{sec:res-reactive}

The second environment removes that objection. Agents trade inside a queue-reactive
simulator calibrated on real per-order Hyperliquid data, in which their market orders
consume visible liquidity, move the price, and meet calibrated refill dynamics. Two
regime-specific calibrations were built, one for calm and one for volatile hours.

In the primary campaign, calm returned a null. Volatile PPO returned a small but genuine
cost signal, $-0.047$\,bps against adaptive TWAP with across-seed $p = 0.006$ --- consistent
in direction across seeds, and yet below the $0.05$\,bps materiality threshold registered
before any agent was trained. A further ninety-eight runs explored the hyperparameter space
and produced a champion configuration.

That champion was then tested twice on blocks sealed until the verdict was fixed, and
\textbf{both tests failed}. The selected configuration returned $-0.0023$\,bps
($p = 0.38$, 3 of 5 seeds cheaper); a second, chosen by the alternative selection rule to
show the outcome was not an artefact of how the champion was picked, returned $-0.0022$\,bps
($p = 0.39$, 2 of 5). Whichever way the winner was selected, it returned approximately zero
on data it had not been chosen on (Table~\ref{tab:t3}).

A second objection now applies, and it is the stronger one. The background price process in
this simulator is unpredictable by construction. Under a pure-cost objective with no
predictable price component, classical execution theory already implies that uniform trading
is close to optimal~\autocite{almgren2001optimal}. The agents may have found nothing because
there was nothing to find.

\section{Performance under injected predictability}\label{sec:res-injected}

The third environment is the study's principal methodological contribution and exists
precisely to close that objection. Rather than assume a signal, the venue's real
short-horizon predictability was measured --- top-of-book queue-depth
imbalance~\autocite{gould2016queue}, which predicts returns over one to ten seconds on this
venue --- and injected into the certified reactive simulator at exactly its measured
strength. The agent is then asked to exploit a signal that is \emph{known} to be present,
which separates ``there was no signal'' from ``the learner failed to find it''.

Figure~\ref{fig:injfid} shows the instrument is faithful: the injected predictability
reproduces the real venue slope within the pre-registered $\pm 20\%$ band at every gated
horizon in both regimes. The environment also passes the same fairness gates as the base
simulator, so no constant-pace policy can profit from residual drift.

The agents were then trained and judged under the identical frozen rule. The result is a
null, and it replicates on \emph{six independent samples}: the development block, a reserve
block, a curve-monitoring block, and a one-shot sealed block, in both regimes. On the sealed
block PPO returned $+0.005$\,bps in calm ($p = 0.63$, 3 of 5 seeds cheaper) and
$+0.025$\,bps in volatile ($p = 0.81$, 2 of 5); neither meets the registered rule
(Table~\ref{tab:sigext-sealed}).

One objection survives even this. Signal present is not the same as signal capturable. A
predictive relationship can be real and still be unreachable given the action set, the
transaction costs, or the horizon.

\section{The attainable edge and the fraction captured}\label{sec:res-ceiling}

This is the central result of the dissertation.

A benchmark policy was registered before the campaign that reads the same injected signal
and does one thing with it: at each decision it requests the pace multiple nearest
$1 + s$, where $s$ is the signal. It has no free parameters, no training, and no memory.
Its logic occupies one line.

On the development block it saved $0.230$\,bps in calm and $0.490$\,bps in volatile against
adaptive TWAP. A single tuned coefficient, fitted on development data and then confirmed
\emph{one-shot} on a freshly minted block, raised this to $\mathbf{0.313}$\,\textbf{bps in
calm} and $\mathbf{0.625}$\,\textbf{bps in volatile}, executing over 98\% of the order in
both cases, with $p < 10^{-25}$ (Table~\ref{tab:sigext-ceiling}). Both figures are five to twelve times
the materiality threshold.

The trained agents, on their own sealed block, captured $-2\%$ and $-4\%$ of that
respectively. Not a small fraction: none of it, and marginally worse than simply trading
uniformly. Figure~\ref{fig:threeenv} places all eighteen pre-registered arms from all three
environments on one axis alongside this comparison, and Figure~\ref{fig:exploiter} shows the
same contrast for the untuned rule family, where every signal-reading rule tested beats the
trained agents.

The conclusion this licenses is narrow and strong. In an environment certified to contain
real, measured, economically material predictability, standard deep reinforcement learning
did not find it, while a rule that could be written on a single line did. The failure is not
an absence of opportunity. It is a failure of learning.

% =====================================================================================
\section{Action-space parity between agents and benchmarks}\label{sec:res-parity}

The obvious rejoinder is that the benchmark won because it had finer control. It did not.
The rule computes a continuous target and then snaps it to the same seven pace multiples the
agents choose from, returning an action index through the identical environment, fill engine
and random-number stream. Every schedule benchmark in the study does likewise.

The single exception is the TWAP benchmark on the replay track, which requests an exact
quantity. That asymmetry was measured rather than argued: the agents' own $1.0\times$ action
reproduces the benchmark to within $0.007$\,bps on the ten-second datasets and $0.023$\,bps
at one minute, on both evaluation periods --- inside the materiality band and two orders of
magnitude below every effect discussed here. The discreteness costs the agent nothing, and
the ceiling in Section~\ref{sec:res-ceiling} was reachable at every decision using actions
the agent had available.

\section{Policy attribution: what the agents learned}\label{sec:res-learned}

\statusbox{Incomplete section. The recorded-book measurements below are final. What is
missing is the same measurement repeated inside the reacting simulator and the injected
environment, which has not been run yet. Nothing already written here changes when it is.}

Research Question~3 asked which order-book signal drives an execution edge, per regime. No
edge was found in any environment, so the question as posed has no subject. It is answered
here in the form the evidence supports: given that the agents did not find the edge, what did
they learn?

Two measurements bear on this. First, the learned value function of every agent in the
injected environment explained approximately zero variance in its own realised returns
(mean explained variance $-0.004$ across ten agents). The critic could not predict the
outcome of the episodes it was trading.

Second, and more directly, agent behaviour is almost entirely reducible to a single number.
Regressing each replay-track agent's per-episode cost against that of a fixed
front-loading rule gives a mean absolute correlation of $0.95$ across thirty agents, with an
intercept indistinguishable from zero. What the agents learned was not a state-conditional
response to order-book information; it was a constant deviation from uniform pacing, which
each seed settled on at a different magnitude.

\emph{Still to be measured: whether the same holds in the two simulated environments,
where a predictable signal is present and there is therefore something for a
state-conditional policy to respond to. That measurement, and its split by market regime,
is outstanding.}

\section{Regime dependence}\label{sec:res-regime}

Research Question~2 asked whether performance depends on market regime, and unlike
Research Question~3 it is answered unconditionally, because it does not require an edge to
exist.

It does depend on regime, in two measured ways. The only cost signal anywhere in the
reactive campaign appeared in volatile conditions ($-0.047$\,bps, across-seed $p = 0.006$)
and was absent in calm --- though, as Section~\ref{sec:res-reactive} records, it sat below
the materiality threshold and did not survive sealed confirmation. Conversely, the DQN
behavioural collapse described in Section~\ref{sec:res-robust} is concentrated in calm
conditions and at the smallest order size, where deferring execution is cheapest.

The asymmetry is interpretable: volatile conditions offer more short-horizon price variation
to time against, while calm conditions make doing nothing nearly free, which is precisely
the setting in which a value-based learner's action values fail to separate.

\section{Sensitivity to order size, deadline and evaluation block}\label{sec:res-robust}

The result does not depend on the order size, the execution deadline, or which block of
market data it was measured on.

A sixteen-cell grid crossing order size with execution deadline produced two apparently
significant cells, both in calm. Both were escalated to additional seeds and then to a
reserve block, and both died (Table~\ref{tab:t4}). This pattern --- a result that clears the
bar on the block it was found on and vanishes on a fresh one --- was demonstrated three
separate times in this study, and Section~\ref{sec:res-apparent} shows a fourth and sharper
instance.

DQN failed differently and consistently. Rather than trading badly, it collapsed
behaviourally: seven of ten agents in the primary campaign, and eight of ten in the injected
environment, deferred execution until the deadline forced completion, and were rejected by
the behaviour audit before any cost was compared. A four-setting probe and an
update-rhythm variant confirmed this is not a tuning artefact.

\section{Comparators and the risk--return frontier}\label{sec:res-comparators}

Two standard comparators were added to confirm the benchmark choice rather than to compete
with it. The Almgren--Chriss schedule at zero urgency is theoretically identical to uniform
trading, and this identity was confirmed empirically in all three environments --- which
demonstrates, rather than asserts, that TWAP is the appropriate primary benchmark under a
pure-cost objective. An oracle VWAP schedule, given perfect foreknowledge of the volume
profile, was more expensive than TWAP in three of four cells and had higher variance in all
four (Table~\ref{tab:sigext-comparators}).

Plotting every feasible policy as a mean-cost against cost-standard-deviation pair
(Figure~\ref{fig:frontier}) places the trained agents strictly inside the efficient frontier
in all five calm seeds and three of five volatile seeds: the agents' deviation from uniform
pacing bought them neither lower cost nor lower risk. The gaps are themselves below the
materiality threshold and are reported as such.

\section{Attribution of an apparent edge on sealed data}\label{sec:res-apparent}

Section~\ref{sec:res-replay} left four configurations unexplained. They are the most
instructive result in the study, and they are a finding about evaluation rather than about
execution.

All four cleared the pre-registered bar on genuinely sealed data. All five seeds agreed in
each. Two cleared a Bonferroni correction for all fourteen configurations outright, at
$p \leq 0.008$. On the ordinary standards of the literature this is a positive result.

It is not one. The same period was equally kind to agents that had already been diagnosed as
non-functional: across all thirty agents on the affected dataset, twenty-eight reversed sign
between the two adjacent periods, including every behaviourally collapsed DQN seed. A learner
that has stopped learning cannot acquire skill, so what changed was the period, not the
policies (Figure~\ref{fig:frozenexam}).

The mechanism was then measured (Figure~\ref{fig:frozenmech}). Because the order is a buy,
TWAP pays the average price across the execution window while a front-loading policy pays
closer to the arrival price; so the sign of within-window price drift determines whether
deviating from uniform pacing is rewarded. That drift reverses between the two periods on
exactly the two datasets whose agents reversed, and not on the third. A fixed rule incapable
of learning reproduces the entire reversal, monotonically in the size of the deviation: at
twice the TWAP pace it costs $+0.236$\,bps in the earlier period and saves $0.548$\,bps in
the later one. Finally, each agent's effective front-loading magnitude is a property of its
weights and is therefore unchanged between periods ($r = 0.999$), while its cost flips ---
and decomposing the observed shift attributes \textbf{95\%} of it to that pacing exposure,
leaving a residual of $-0.025$\,bps, half the materiality threshold.

Two predictions could have refuted this and did not. The first is directional: an agent that
front-loads should become cheaper on the later period, and an agent that delays should become
more expensive. This holds for \textbf{all thirty agents without exception} --- twenty-six
front-loaders all fall, four delayers all rise --- so the sign of a single number computed
from an agent's weights predicts which way its measured cost will move. The second is
degenerate: one collapsed agent returns a front-loading magnitude of exactly $1.000$ with a
zero intercept and unit correlation. It has ceased to be a learner and become the
maximum-pace benchmark, so its movement is definitionally a property of the period.

Why the two adjacent periods differ in within-window drift is a fact about Bitcoin in 2025
that this study does not attempt to explain. The claim here is about what such a difference
does to a benchmark comparison, and it is a sharp one: a result can pass a genuine sealed
hold-out, with unanimous seed agreement and a small $p$-value, and still be almost entirely a
market exposure that any fixed rule would have collected. Out-of-sample evaluation and seed
agreement are each necessary; together they are still not sufficient.

\section{Dependence of the result on the observation specification}\label{sec:res-obs}

\statusbox{Incomplete section. The result reported below is final. Three further sets of
training runs, whose predictions were fixed in writing before they began, are still being
scored; they extend the same test to other network settings, to the second algorithm, and to
the reacting simulator that carries the central claim.}

Agents were scored on their execution price relative to the price at order arrival, while the
arrival price itself was not among the state variables they observed. Whether the null
depends on that specification is therefore a question the study must answer rather than
assume, and it was tested directly.

Adding the missing variable to the observation of ten agents in the injected environment
raised the critic's explained variance from $-0.004$ to $+0.405$: the value function moved
from predicting nothing to predicting well. Execution cost was unchanged
(Table~\ref{tab:sigext-a4}, Figure~\ref{fig:obs}).

\emph{Still to be scored: eighteen runs across three network and reward settings,
establishing whether the finding is specific to one configuration; ten DQN runs, which also
test whether the behavioural collapse reported in
Section~\ref{sec:res-robust} is partly attributable to the same specification; and twenty
runs repeating the primary reacting-market campaign under the corrected observation, which
extends the test from the injected environment to the one carrying the central claim.}
